A · The big picture

Why Slay the Spire as a planning benchmark?

Most LLM evaluations are static question-answering. Planning is different: it requires sequencing actions under a resource budget, committing to a strategy that pays off many decisions later, and reasoning about consequences that unfold over time. Slay the Spire demands all three — yet it is small enough to simulate exactly and score against ground truth.

The game gives us a natural ladder of planning horizons: a single turn (seconds of foresight), a full combat (a few minutes), deck-building synergy (strategic abstraction), and an entire 15-floor run (sustained consistency). Each rung becomes one experiment, so we can locate exactly where a model's planning ability breaks down rather than producing one opaque score.

B · Shared method

The same engine underlies every experiment

Three design choices are common to all four experiments and worth stating once:

  • Deterministic simulator. A faithful Python re-implementation of the combat engine, map traversal, card drafting, and enemy AI. Nine independent seeded RNG streams mean a single seed fixes the entire run — every experiment is exactly reproducible.
  • Two prompt formats, identical seeds. Every model is tested in structured (compact JSON) and raw (natural-English) form on the same underlying state, so any score gap is attributable to format alone — a clean ablation.
  • Explicit ground truth per dimension. Turn = exhaustive search; combat = greedy-bot baseline; synergy = hand-crafted archetype decks; run = absolute survival. No human grading.

Experiment 1

1Turn-level planning

Motivation

The shortest planning horizon. Within a single turn, the order in which cards are played changes the outcome — block before an attack, apply vulnerability before damage, spend energy efficiently. If a model cannot optimise one turn, longer horizons are hopeless. This is the floor of the ladder.

Research question

Given a hand, energy, and enemy intent, can the model choose the card sequence that maximises damage — and does it stay within the rules (only legal plays)?

Implementation

For each sampled turn we exhaustively search all legal card orderings (≤720 permutations) to find the true optimum. The model proposes a sequence; we score damage ratio (its damage ÷ optimal) and legal rate (fraction of proposals containing only legal plays). Any illegal card in the sequence scores the whole turn 0 — partial credit would reward guessing. n=5 turns.

Result

Mid-range and highly format-sensitive. The small model nearly doubles its damage ratio in natural English (36.7%→69.6%) and goes from 60%→100% legal. The larger model is more consistent on legality but does not dominate on damage.

Experiment 2

2Combat-level planning

Motivation

A real fight spans many turns: the model must manage HP, react to changing enemy intents, and trade block against damage across the whole encounter — not just optimise one snapshot. This tests sustained tactical play.

Research question

Can the model win a full combat, and does it play efficiently — i.e. does it finish with at least as much HP as a simple greedy baseline bot?

Implementation

The model plays a fight turn-by-turn against a scripted enemy until win or death. We record win rate and HP ratio (model's remaining HP ÷ the greedy bot's, on the identical fight). A ratio > 100% means it outplayed the baseline. n=3 combats.

Result

Saturated. Every model, both formats, wins 100% of fights and beats the greedy bot on HP (103–114%). Single-combat tactics are not where LLM planning struggles — the difficulty lives at the longer horizons below.

Experiment 3

3Synergy & archetype reasoning

Motivation

Strong play in Slay the Spire is not card-by-card — it is committing to a strategy (an archetype) and shaping the deck toward it: drafting cards that fit, cutting cards that dilute it. This is the most abstract, human-like planning skill in the game.

Research question

Given a deck, can the model (a) identify its archetype, (b) pick the best card to add, and (c) choose the right card to remove? In particular: does it grasp the meta-principle that basic cards should be cut because they dilute draw quality?

Implementation

Earlier versions drafted decks randomly, but Act-1 decks are too small to have a crisp archetype. So synergy now uses 8 hand-crafted decks — two each for Strength, Block, Exhaust, and Aggro — each with 4–5 unmistakable signature cards, a basic Strike as the obvious removal target, and a 3-card offer whose on-archetype card is the expert pick. Ground truth is deterministic. We score archetype accuracy, card-pick accuracy, and removal accuracy. n=8 (one pass over all fixtures).

Result

The richest experiment. Three distinct, reproducible findings emerged — see the callouts below the tables.

Finding 3a

Models cannot recognise the Exhaust archetype. All eight Exhaust decks — across both models and both formats — were labelled "Aggro" (0/8), despite being full of Exhaust signature cards. Strength was nearly as weak (2/8). Models name an archetype reliably only when its signature is a simple surface pattern (lots of attacks → Aggro; lots of block → Block); they miss strategies defined by a mechanical interaction (exhausting cards for a payoff). A clean, systematic knowledge gap.

Finding 3b

Naming the strategy and playing it are dissociated. Card-pick accuracy stays high (62.5–100%) even on decks the model cannot label — llama-8b (structured) picks the correct card 100% of the time while scoring only 50% on archetype ID. Local card-quality judgement is strong; the abstract strategic label is weak. The model often makes the right move for reasons it cannot articulate.

Finding 3c

Card removal is a near-total failure (12.5–25%). Expert play removes a basic Strike first — basics dilute draw quality as the deck improves. Models instead propose cutting situational cards (Disarm, Battle Trance, Bash), reasoning about a card's standalone quality rather than the deck-cycling principle. The small non-zero scores come only from Block decks where the targets happen to coincide. A systematic conceptual gap, not noise.

Experiment 4

4Run-level planning

Motivation

The top of the ladder: an entire 15-floor act plus boss, where every combat, path choice, and draft compounds. This is where planning is genuinely hard — local mistakes accumulate into death.

Research question

Can the model survive a full Act 1 — making consistent, mutually-coherent decisions as state builds up over dozens of choices?

Implementation

The model drives a full run floor-by-floor (combat, events, rewards, drafting). We score survival rate, floors reached, and draft coherence.

Result

No valid data yet. The run-level numbers currently on disk were produced before three correctness fixes (map dead-end, event-bus listener stacking, null-index handling) and would misrepresent ability, so they are excluded entirely. A clean pass is the first priority — currently blocked by free-tier provider rate limits (run-level is by far the most token-heavy dimension). See §E.

C · Cross-cutting findings

Difficulty lives upstream

Combat is saturated (100% win, beats the bot), yet the more abstract synergy reasoning collapses on whole archetypes. The hard part of planning is not winning a fight — it is the strategic, longer-horizon reasoning.

Prompt format is a real lever — and task-dependent

Natural-English prompts help reasoning: llama-8b's turn damage ratio nearly doubles (36.7%→69.6%) and legality rises 60%→100% in raw. But structured JSON can help index-based output: scout-17b identifies archetypes better in structured (75% vs 50%). Which format wins depends on the task — not a wash, and worth controlling for in any future LLM-planning study.

D · Limitations

  • Sample size. n=3–8 per dimension. Numbers are directional; no significance claimed. (Synergy's n=8 is a fixed deterministic set — clean signal, but no sampling variance to compute a std over.)
  • Single seed. All runs use seed=42; multiple seeds are needed to rule out RNG artefacts.
  • Model breadth. Two models, both Llama-family. A cross-family ladder — ideally with a reasoning model — is needed for a capability story.
  • No run-level data. The hardest dimension currently has no valid measurement.
  • Reasoning model deferred. A 32B reasoning model was attempted but could not run on free-tier providers (rate caps truncate its reasoning; the alternative was too slow). Needs a paid tier.

None of these are harness defects — the simulator, ground truth, and scoring are paper-ready and fully tested. The remaining work is scale and breadth of runs — compute-bound, not code-bound.
