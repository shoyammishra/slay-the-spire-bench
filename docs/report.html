1. What this project is

slay-bench asks a concrete question: how well can a language model actually plan? Not on multiple-choice trivia, but inside a game that demands sequencing actions under a resource budget, building a deck over dozens of decisions, and thinking several steps ahead.

The project has two halves. First, a Python simulator that faithfully recreates the game's combat engine, map traversal, card drafting, and enemy AI — fully deterministic from a single seed, so every experiment is exactly reproducible. Second, a benchmark harness that renders the game state into a prompt, collects the model's decision, and scores it against ground truth across four levels of planning horizon.

2. Benchmark design

Each dimension isolates a different planning horizon and is scored against an explicit ground truth:

#DimensionWhat it testsGround truth
1Turn-levelBest card sequence within one turnExhaustive search (≤720 permutations)
2Combat-levelWin a full fight, turn by turnGreedy-bot baseline
3SynergyIdentify deck archetype; pick best card; remove worst card8 hand-crafted archetype decks
4Run-levelSurvive all 15 floors + boss of Act 1Absolute (survival + progress)

Two prompt formats — a controlled ablation

Every model is tested twice on identical seeds: structured (compact JSON: card names, costs, HP in dicts) and raw (verbose natural-English description of the same state). Because the underlying RNG is identical, any score difference is attributable to format alone.

Why a hand-crafted synergy set. Earlier versions drafted decks randomly, but Act-1 decks are too small and RNG-limited to have a crisp archetype (those crystallise in Acts 2–3). The synergy dimension now uses 8 fixed, hand-built decks — two per archetype (Strength, Block, Exhaust, Aggro), each with 4–5 unmistakable signature cards, a basic Strike as the obvious removal target, and a 3-card offer whose on-archetype card is the expert pick. Ground truth is therefore deterministic and unambiguous.

5. Key findings

Finding 1 — Models cannot recognise the Exhaust archetype. Across every model and every format, all eight Exhaust decks were labelled "Aggro" (0/8). Strength decks fared little better (2/8). Models reliably name an archetype only when its signature is a simple, common pattern (Block, generic Aggro) — they miss strategies defined by a mechanical interaction (exhausting cards for payoff) even when the deck is stuffed with signature cards. This is a clean, systematic knowledge gap, not random noise.
Finding 2 — Naming the strategy and playing it are dissociated. Even on decks whose archetype it could not name, llama-8b (structured) picked the correct best card 100% of the time, and scout-17b (raw) likewise hit 100% card-pick. In other words, the models choose the right card for the deck while being unable to articulate why — local card-quality judgement is strong; the abstract strategic label is weak.
Finding 3 — Card removal is a near-total failure (12–25%). The expert heuristic removes a basic Strike first — basics dilute draw quality as the deck improves. Models instead almost always propose removing a situational card (Disarm, Battle Trance, Bash), reasoning about a card's standalone quality rather than the deck-cycling principle that makes basics the right cut. A systematic conceptual gap with clear pedagogical value for the paper.
Finding 4 — Combat competence is saturated; the difficulty lives upstream. Both models win 100% of isolated fights and beat the greedy bot on remaining HP (103–114%). The hard part of planning is not winning one fight — it is the longer-horizon consistency (deck-building, archetype reasoning) where scores drop sharply.
Finding 5 — Prompt format matters, and the better format depends on the task. Natural-English prompts help reasoning: llama-8b's turn damage ratio nearly doubles (36.7%→69.6%) and its legal-play rate rises 60%→100% in raw. But structured JSON helps index-based output: scout-17b identifies archetypes far better in structured (75% vs 50%). Format is a real, measurable lever — not a wash.

6. Limitations

  • Sample size. n=3–8 per dimension. Numbers are directional; no statistical significance is claimed. (Synergy n=8 is a fixed deterministic set, so it has no sampling variance — a clean mini-exam rather than a distribution.)
  • Single seed. All runs use seed=42 — one fixed map/enemy/draw layout. Multiple seeds are needed to rule out RNG artefacts.
  • Model breadth. Two models, both from the Llama family. A cross-family ladder (and ideally a reasoning model) is needed for a capability story.
  • No run-level data. The hardest dimension currently has no valid measurement.
  • Reasoning model deferred. A 32B reasoning model (qwen3) was attempted but could not be run on free-tier providers (token-per-minute caps truncate its reasoning; the alternative tier was too slow). It needs a paid tier — left as future work.

None of these are harness defects — the simulator, ground truth, and scoring are paper-ready and the code is fully tested. The remaining work is scale and breadth of runs, which is compute-bound, not code-bound.

7. Next steps to paper-grade

A concrete, checklist-style run matrix:

Scale

  • n ≥ 20–30 per dimension per model
  • ≥ 5 seeds (e.g. 42, 142, 242, 342, 442)
  • Both formats on identical seeds
  • Report mean ± std / 95% CI, no point estimates

Breadth

  • Add a 3rd model from another family (cross-family signal)
  • Optionally a reasoning model on a paid tier
  • Run-level first — it has no valid data and is the longest pole
  • Then expand the synergy fixture set for error bars
The single blocker is provider throughput: the run-heavy dimension exhausts free-tier rate limits. A paid, higher-throughput tier unblocks the entire matrix above in one pass.
