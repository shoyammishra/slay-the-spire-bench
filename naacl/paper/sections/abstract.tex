\begin{abstract}
Ask a language model for the move that pays off eight steps from now, and it may give you the move that pays off right away. Existing planning benchmarks cannot tell these apart, because they change the problem whenever they change the horizon. We built a test that holds everything fixed except the stated horizon. Each item is a frozen state from a turn-based card game, posed with a goal one, two, four or eight decisions ahead. Exhaustive search gives the provably best move at every horizon, and the scoring gives no credit for ignoring the instruction. Eight open models from four families fall into three groups. Small models ignore the horizon. Larger models, with or without explicit reasoning, abandon the obvious move. Only the two strongest reasoning models also pick the better long-term move more often, gpt-oss-120b on both characters and Qwen3-32B reliably on one. Even their average answer quality does not improve. They find better moves on some states but give up correct ones on others, and they lose ground where the obvious move was already right. Switching off one model's reasoning trace removes most of this response. An exact rulebook or hidden card names do not explain this pattern. Models treat ``look further ahead'' as a cue to change their answer, without optimizing for the stated goal. The test needs only a deterministic simulator. In a small chess pilot, gpt-oss-120b again changes its moves without a significant gain in score. We release the states, the exact answers and every model response.
\end{abstract}
