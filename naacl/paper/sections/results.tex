\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Seven of the eight runs pass both validity gates. Qwen3-8B cut off 4.0\% of its answers at the token limit, so its numbers are descriptive only.

\subsection{The models can play the short game}

A model that cannot play at all would also fail to plan, so we first check play at $H{=}1$. On the sensitive states, every model except Mistral-7B picks the best one-decision move well above the random rate. Most do so about twice as often (Table~\ref{tab:competence}). gpt-oss finds it in 60\% of Ironclad sensitive states, against 22\% for a random move. Told $H{=}8$, the same models on the same states pick the best eight-decision move at close to the random rate. The failure is specific to the longer horizon. Mistral-7B is the exception at both horizons, because half of its answers are illegal.

\subsection{Three tiers of response}

The models fall into three tiers by how they respond to the horizon.

\paragraph{Small models do not react.} Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-7B give nearly the same answer whatever horizon they are told, changing 48 and 59 of their 1,760 answers between $H{=}1$ and $H{=}8$. Mistral-7B changes 307, mostly from illegal answers to legal ones. Qwen3-8B changes more than half of its answers, but its changes run in no direction. It gains about as many best moves as it loses, on both kinds of state (Figure~\ref{fig:churn}).

\paragraph{Larger models leave the greedy move.} Llama-3.3-70B and Qwen3-14B abandon the greedy move often when told $H{=}8$. On the 880 sensitive states, Llama-3.3-70B plays it 425 times at $H{=}1$ and 351 times at $H{=}8$, and Qwen3-14B goes from 378 to 252. Neither picks the long-horizon move more often than its own $H{=}1$ answer does (Figure~\ref{fig:use}). Reacting to the horizon does not require explicit reasoning, since Llama-3.3-70B does not reason step by step.

\paragraph{Two models also find the long-horizon move.} Qwen3-32B and gpt-oss-120b choose a best eight-decision move more often at $H{=}8$ than at $H{=}1$. For gpt-oss the rates are 36\% against 25\% (Ironclad) and 31\% against 21\% (Silent). For Qwen3-32B they are 28\% against 22\% and 30\% against 19\%. McNemar's test gives $p \le .026$ in all four cases, and the gap holds at every horizon we tested (Appendix~\ref{app:curves}). For comparison, a random legal move is a best eight-decision move 24\% of the time. With Holm correction over all 16 model and character tests, three of the four gains survive. The fourth, Qwen3-32B on Ironclad, does not (corrected $p=.34$), and it is also not significant on the 1,169 clean states. We therefore treat it as weak evidence.

\subsection{Responding is not planning}

Finding the long-horizon move more often does not make the answers better on average. Table~\ref{tab:primary} splits each model's gain into sensitive and control states. On sensitive states, where looking ahead should pay, no responsive model improves reliably. On control states, where the greedy move is already right, every responsive model gets worse. gpt-oss loses .14 on Ironclad controls and .11 on Silent controls. The pre-registered primary statistic, sensitive gain minus control gain, is significant for three models, and in each case the control loss produces it. We therefore read the two columns side by side and never the difference alone.

Figure~\ref{fig:churn} shows why the averages do not move. The instruction makes the responsive models change most of their answers, and the changes cut both ways. On sensitive states, gpt-oss turns 189 answers into a best move but gives up 98 best moves it already had. Its other changes land on moves worse than the ones they replace, so its sensitive-state score stays flat. On control states the balance tips the wrong way: 77 best moves found and 284 given up. Qwen3-32B, Qwen3-14B and Llama-3.3-70B show the same pattern on a smaller scale.

The changes are not random either. When gpt-oss abandons its greedy $H{=}1$ answer, the new move is a best eight-decision move 46\% of the time on Ironclad and 38\% on Silent. Picking uniformly among the other legal moves would give 32\% and 31\%. Qwen3-32B (43\% and 40\%) and Qwen3-14B (47\% and 33\%) also beat this chance level. Most switched answers are skills or powers, the cards that defend or set up later turns: 77\% for gpt-oss and 66\% for Qwen3-32B. The instruction pushes models toward setup moves in general, whether or not setup pays in the state at hand. The same holds on the sensitive states where a model's $H{=}1$ answer was the best one-decision move. On these states the model had read the position correctly. There, Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-7B keep their answer 97\% to 99\% of the time. gpt-oss moves to the long-horizon move on 30\% of these states and to some other non-best move on 35\% (Ironclad) and 48\% (Silent) (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).

A trivial rule puts these numbers in perspective. Playing the leftmost playable card scores .66 (Ironclad) and .69 (Silent) on sensitive states. At $H{=}8$, no model's answers on those states score more than .06 above this rule.

The state in Figure~\ref{fig:example} shows the pattern in one case. At $H{=}1$, gpt-oss plays Pommel Strike, the best move, and explains that it ``deals the most damage this turn.'' At $H{=}8$ it switches to Feel No Pain, a power that grants block whenever a card is later exhausted, reasoning that it ``sets up future block generation.'' The switch sounds like planning, but the move is worth .53 on the eight-decision scale. Bash, which keeps the boss Vulnerable, is worth 1. Qwen3-32B plays Bash at both horizons.

\subsection{The reasoning trace drives the response}

Qwen3 can switch its reasoning trace off with one request flag, so we reran Qwen3-32B with the same weights and thinking disabled. This run was frozen as Amendment 8 after the thinking-on results were known, so we treat it as a post hoc test. Without thinking, the model changes 15\% of its answers between $H{=}1$ and $H{=}8$, against 66\% with thinking (Figure~\ref{fig:churn}). Its gain in long-horizon picks at $H{=}8$ disappears: 22\% against 20\% on Ironclad ($p=.052$) and 25\% against 24\% on Silent (Figure~\ref{fig:use}). When it does leave the greedy move, it lands on the long-horizon move at about the chance rate, 32\% against 29\%. Small gains remain at $H{=}2$ and $H{=}4$ (Appendix~\ref{app:curves}), and so does a smaller loss on control states. In this model the reasoning trace produces most of the response to the horizon, both the extra long-horizon picks and most of the lost correct answers.

\subsection{It is not about knowing the game}

Our prompts name each card without describing it, so a model must recall what ``Bash'' does. A reviewer could fairly ask whether the horizon results reflect gaps in game knowledge, or recognition of famous card names, rather than planning. We tested both explanations in a pre-registered amendment on the 1,169 states that hold no targeted skill card (see Limitations). In the \emph{described} condition, every prompt carries an exact rulebook generated from the simulator: what each visible card does, how each enemy behaves and how every effect works. In the \emph{renamed} condition, every card, enemy and relic name, and the name of the game, is replaced by a neutral code such as ``Card K7''. We ran both conditions on one model that ignores the horizon (Llama-3.1-8B) and one that responds to it (gpt-oss-120b).

None of the eight pre-registered contrasts survives Holm correction \citep{holm1979} (Table~\ref{tab:knowledge}). For Llama-3.1-8B the null is confirmed. Equivalence tests \citep{schuirmann1987tost} place all four of its contrasts within the pre-registered smallest effect of interest, $\pm.10$. With the rulebook it still ignores the horizon, so its blind spot is not a knowledge gap. For gpt-oss the evidence is weaker. With the rulebook its primary statistic rises by .073 on both characters. This is not significant after correction, but effects up to about .14 cannot be excluded. The rise comes from larger losses on control states. Its use of the long-horizon move does not change reliably. With all names hidden it still responds to the horizon, so recognizing famous cards is not what drives it.\footnote{gpt-oss cut off 1.9\% of its renamed-condition answers, above the 1\% gate, so its familiarity contrasts are descriptive.}

\subsection{Robustness}

Every conclusion above holds when any one of the five encounters is removed, under a bootstrap that resamples states within each encounter, and on the 1,169 states that the oracle limitation described under Limitations leaves untouched (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). The only change on the clean states is the weak Qwen3-32B Ironclad gain discussed above. All four significant primary statistics survive Holm correction over the 14 valid model and character tests. Measured in raw value, without normalization, the control-state change of every responsive model is negative in sign. It is significant only for Qwen3-14B on Ironclad, gpt-oss on Silent and the no-thinking run on Ironclad. The win and loss bonuses make raw values heavy-tailed, which widens the intervals. Conditioning on a correct $H{=}1$ answer, and the clean-state results, are in Appendix~\ref{app:robust}. A prompt variant that defines ``decision transition'' explicitly changes nothing for either model tested (Appendix~\ref{app:amendments}). These models' scores on full fights and full runs in the same simulator do not predict their horizon use. Those scores sit at a ceiling for fights and a floor for runs, which is why a controlled test is needed.
